# Proposal Templates

**Status:** Phase 2 working template system  
**Owner:** Founder  
**Last updated:** 2026-09-27

## 1. Purpose

This document defines the canonical structure for client proposals.

A proposal should turn a qualified client need into a clear, reviewable statement of:

- the problem being addressed;
- the proposed deliverables;
- the boundaries of the work;
- the client's inputs and responsibilities;
- the delivery approach;
- acceptance conditions;
- commercial terms from approved pricing;
- the next step.

A proposal is not a substitute for a detailed project agreement when the project requires one.

## 2. Proposal source hierarchy

Use these sources in this order:

1. Founder-approved business decisions
2. Approved service definition
3. Qualified client requirements
4. Verified portfolio/profile evidence
5. Reusable proposal language from this document

A proposal must not introduce claims that conflict with higher-authority sources.

## 3. Master proposal structure

Use this structure for a new proposal.

### Proposal title

Proposal — [Project / Service Name]

### Client context

Client: [Client name]  
Prepared for: [Contact name, if applicable]  
Prepared by: [Business / Founder]  
Date: [YYYY-MM-DD]  
Validity: [Only when supported by approved commercial policy]

### 1. Understanding of the need

Summarize the client's stated problem, desired outcome, and relevant constraints.

Write this from the qualified discovery information. Do not invent requirements.

### 2. Proposed solution

Explain the proposed web solution in plain language.

Connect the solution to the client's need rather than listing technology for its own sake.

### 3. Scope of work

#### Included

- [Deliverable]
- [Deliverable]
- [Deliverable]

#### Excluded

- [Explicit exclusion]
- [Explicit exclusion]
- [Out-of-scope request or dependency]

Scope must be concrete enough that both parties can identify whether a requested item is included.

### 4. Deliverables

For each major deliverable, state what will be provided.

Example format:

Deliverable: [Name]  
Description: [What will be delivered]  
Acceptance: [How completion will be recognized]

### 5. Approach

Describe the relevant stages, for example:

1. Confirm requirements and inputs
2. Implement the agreed structure and interface
3. Review and test
4. Apply agreed revisions
5. Complete handover

Adapt the sequence to the actual project.

### 6. Client responsibilities

List required client inputs such as:

- content and approvals;
- brand assets;
- existing-site access;
- account/integration access;
- legal or compliance text;
- timely feedback;
- other project-specific dependencies.

Do not assign responsibilities the client has not accepted.

### 7. Timeline and milestones

Use only a timeline supported by the actual scope, dependencies, and current capacity.

Target start: [Date / subject to agreement]  
Milestone 1: [Description]  
Milestone 2: [Description]  
Handover: [Description]

Do not promise a fixed delivery date when required client inputs or dependencies are unresolved.

### 8. Revisions and change control

State the agreed revision boundary.

Material requests outside the approved scope should be recorded and agreed before implementation.

Do not promise unlimited revisions unless the Founder has explicitly approved that commercial structure for the proposal.

### 9. Quality and verification

Describe the checks appropriate to the project, such as:

- responsive behavior;
- primary user flows;
- browser/device checks;
- accessibility;
- performance;
- security;
- integrations.

Only claim checks that will actually be performed.

### 10. Commercial terms

Commercial amounts, payment milestones, validity periods, and other financial terms must be populated only from Founder-approved pricing and commercial policy.

Do not invent pricing inside a reusable template.

### 11. Assumptions and dependencies

Record material assumptions such as:

- client content is supplied by an agreed date;
- third-party accounts remain available;
- external services operate within their own limits;
- required approvals are provided on time.

Material assumptions should be visible before acceptance.

### 12. Acceptance and handover

Define what constitutes delivery and what the client receives at handover.

Possible items include:

- completed agreed pages/features;
- source or repository handover where applicable;
- deployment information where included;
- basic usage notes;
- known limitations or remaining agreed items.

### 13. Next step

Use one concrete next action:

To proceed, confirm that the scope and commercial terms are acceptable and provide the required project inputs.

Adapt this to the actual sales process.

## 4. Service-specific proposal prompts

Use the relevant service definition to shape the proposal.

### Business website

Clarify:

- page/site structure;
- responsive behavior;
- content responsibilities;
- required forms or integrations;
- deployment responsibility;
- handover expectations.

### Landing page

Clarify:

- target audience;
- offer;
- primary call to action;
- required sections;
- analytics/integration requirements;
- content and approval responsibility.

### Website redesign / modernization

Clarify:

- current-site problems;
- pages or flows included;
- what will remain;
- migration/content responsibilities;
- before/after evidence where appropriate;
- modernization boundaries.

### Custom web application

Clarify:

- user roles;
- workflows;
- functional boundaries;
- data/integration requirements;
- acceptance criteria;
- hosting/deployment responsibilities;
- security and access assumptions.

### Dashboard / portal

Clarify:

- user types;
- data sources;
- views/workflows;
- permissions;
- integrations;
- access and security responsibilities;
- acceptance conditions.

### Website maintenance / improvements

Clarify:

- exact fixes or improvements;
- affected areas;
- dependencies;
- testing expectations;
- whether the engagement is one-time or recurring, if approved;
- what is explicitly outside the requested maintenance scope.

## 5. Proposal writing rules

Prefer:

- specific scope;
- plain language;
- observable deliverables;
- explicit assumptions;
- measurable acceptance conditions;
- honest limitations.

Avoid:

- unsupported superlatives;
- guaranteed revenue, leads, conversions, rankings, or traffic;
- fabricated experience;
- fabricated client examples;
- invented urgency;
- vague promises such as "everything you need";
- technology lists that do not explain the client value;
- commercial numbers not backed by approved pricing.

## 6. Proposal review checklist

Before sending a proposal:

- [ ] Client need matches the qualified information.
- [ ] Service category matches the approved service catalog.
- [ ] Scope is specific and bounded.
- [ ] Exclusions are visible.
- [ ] Deliverables have acceptance conditions.
- [ ] Client responsibilities are realistic and explicit.
- [ ] Timeline does not ignore unresolved dependencies.
- [ ] Revision/change boundaries are clear.
- [ ] Quality checks match the actual project.
- [ ] Commercial terms use approved pricing only.
- [ ] No confidential client information is copied unnecessarily.
- [ ] No unsupported claims are included.
- [ ] Next step is clear.
- [ ] Proposal has been reviewed before external sending.

## 7. Proposal lifecycle

Qualified need → Draft proposal → Internal review → Client proposal → Clarification/revision → Acceptance → Delivery handoff

The proposal should preserve the same scope and facts when accepted. Material changes after proposal acceptance should be recorded through the agreed change-control process.
