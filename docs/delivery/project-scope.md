# Project Scope

**Status:** Phase 3 working delivery process  
**Owner:** Founder  
**Last updated:** 2026-09-27

## 1. Purpose

Scope turns the accepted commercial agreement and completed discovery into a precise, reviewable definition of what the project will deliver.

A project scope should make it possible for the delivery owner and client to answer:

- what is included;
- what is excluded;
- what each deliverable means;
- how completion will be recognized;
- what the client must provide;
- what dependencies or assumptions apply;
- what happens when a requested change falls outside the agreed scope.

Scope protects both delivery clarity and commercial boundaries. It is not permission to add work that was not accepted.

## 2. Entry conditions

Prepare the detailed project scope only when:

- the project has reached the delivery handoff;
- the accepted proposal and commercial scope are available;
- Discovery is complete enough to identify material requirements;
- important dependencies and client inputs are visible;
- unresolved questions that would materially change scope have an owner and next action.

Do not finalize scope while material requirements remain unknown unless the scope explicitly records the uncertainty and its effect.

## 3. Scope authority and source hierarchy

Use these sources in order:

1. Founder-approved business and delivery rules
2. Accepted proposal and approved commercial scope
3. Approved discovery decisions and clarifications
4. Approved service definition for the selected service
5. Verified project requirements and client inputs
6. Implementation detail

For a specific requirement, an approved discovery clarification may replace the earlier requirement it resolves. It does not by itself authorize a material commercial scope expansion, reduction, or change in responsibility.

When the requested work cannot be reconciled with the accepted commercial scope, stop and use the agreed change-control process before implementation.

## 4. Scope objectives

A complete scope should establish:

1. Project objective
2. Included work
3. Explicit exclusions
4. Deliverables
5. Acceptance criteria
6. Client responsibilities
7. Assumptions
8. Dependencies
9. Constraints
10. Planned handoffs or milestone inputs
11. Open items that must not be mistaken for committed scope

The scope should be specific enough that a reasonable reviewer can determine whether a request is already included.

## 5. Scope structure

### 5.1 Project context

Record:

- Project ID
- Client / organization
- Service category
- Scope owner
- Scope version
- Scope status
- Date prepared
- Date approved, when applicable

Summarize the agreed project objective in plain language.

### 5.2 Included work

List the actual work covered by the accepted project.

Use concrete items such as:

- page or screen groups;
- features;
- workflows;
- content or migration tasks;
- integrations;
- responsive behavior;
- configuration;
- agreed deployment or handover tasks.

Avoid vague phrases such as "complete website" or "all required functionality."

### 5.3 Excluded work

List material exclusions explicitly.

Typical exclusions may include:

- pages, screens, or workflows not listed as included;
- new integrations discovered after scope approval;
- unapproved content creation;
- brand identity work;
- paid third-party services or licenses;
- ongoing operational support not included in the engagement;
- business outcomes such as guaranteed traffic, leads, revenue, or conversions.

Use the approved proposal and service definition rather than inventing exclusions.

### 5.4 Deliverables

For every major deliverable, record:

- Deliverable name
- Description
- Included components
- Dependencies
- Acceptance criteria
- Handover item, when applicable

A deliverable should describe an observable result, not only an implementation task.

### 5.5 Acceptance criteria

Acceptance criteria should state how the client and delivery owner will recognize that the agreed deliverable is complete.

Use observable conditions, for example:

- required pages or screens exist;
- specified workflow can be completed;
- agreed integration behaves as documented;
- agreed content is present;
- responsive behavior meets the stated project requirement;
- agreed handover material is provided.

Avoid criteria that depend on guaranteed business outcomes.

### 5.6 Client responsibilities

Record the client responsibilities that materially affect delivery, such as:

- content and approvals;
- brand assets;
- account or system access;
- integration credentials provided through an approved secure method;
- legal or compliance text;
- data or migration inputs;
- timely feedback;
- final acceptance.

Only assign responsibilities that are supported by the accepted proposal, service definition, or approved project decision.

### 5.7 Assumptions

Record assumptions that materially affect scope or delivery, such as:

- client inputs are supplied by an agreed point;
- existing systems remain available;
- third-party services remain operational within their limits;
- approved content does not materially change during implementation.

An assumption that proves false may require a scope or schedule review.

### 5.8 Dependencies

Record external or internal conditions that must be satisfied, such as:

- third-party API availability;
- domain or hosting access;
- client account permissions;
- content delivery;
- approval from another stakeholder;
- migration source availability.

Dependencies should have an owner where practical.

### 5.9 Constraints

Record known boundaries such as:

- required platforms or browsers;
- data or security constraints;
- regulatory or contractual constraints;
- existing-system compatibility;
- fixed launch dependencies;
- approved technology or infrastructure constraints, when they are genuinely part of the project.

Do not introduce implementation preferences as contractual constraints unless they are approved.

### 5.10 Open items

Open items are not automatically scope.

For each material open item, record:

- question or missing information;
- owner;
- required-by point, when known;
- current status;
- scope impact if unresolved.

Do not implement an open item as committed work without the necessary approval.

## 6. Scope sizing and boundaries

The scope should be bounded at the level appropriate to the service.

For a business website or landing page, the scope may be organized around pages, sections, content responsibilities, forms, integrations, responsive behavior, and handover.

For a redesign or modernization project, distinguish retained material, revised material, migration work, and explicitly excluded legacy areas.

For a custom web application or dashboard/portal, define roles, workflows, key screens, data sources, integrations, permissions, and acceptance conditions.

For maintenance/improvements, identify the exact affected areas and requested changes rather than treating the engagement as unlimited maintenance.

## 7. Scope traceability

Each major scope item should be traceable to its source.

Use a simple source label such as:

- Proposal
- Discovery decision
- Service definition
- Approved project decision
- Client input

Traceability should make it possible to explain why an item is included without exposing unnecessary confidential client material in shared business documentation.

## 8. Change control

A requested change should be treated as a scope change when it:

- adds a new deliverable;
- expands a workflow materially;
- adds a new integration;
- adds a new role or permission model;
- changes an acceptance condition materially;
- adds migration, content, infrastructure, or operational work not already included;
- changes the client's or delivery owner's agreed responsibilities.

Before implementing a material out-of-scope request:

1. Describe the requested change.
2. Identify the affected scope items.
3. Identify delivery, dependency, or acceptance impact.
4. Record any commercial or timeline impact using approved policy.
5. Obtain the required agreement or approval.
6. Update the scope and related milestone/QA records.
7. Preserve the previous scope version in the client project record.

Do not use scope updates to silently rewrite history.

## 9. Scope versioning

Use explicit versions for approved scope records:

- Draft — still being prepared
- Review — awaiting client/internal review
- Approved — agreed basis for delivery
- Superseded — replaced by a later approved scope version
- Closed — final historical record after project completion

Each approved revision should identify:

- version;
- date;
- change summary;
- approval source;
- affected deliverables.

## 11. Scope checklist

Before marking scope Approved:

- [ ] Accepted proposal has been reviewed.
- [ ] Discovery record is Ready for scope.
- [ ] Included work is concrete and bounded.
- [ ] Material exclusions are explicit.
- [ ] Major deliverables have observable acceptance criteria.
- [ ] Client responsibilities are identified.
- [ ] Material assumptions are recorded.
- [ ] Material dependencies are recorded with owners where practical.
- [ ] Constraints are visible.
- [ ] Open items are clearly separated from committed scope.
- [ ] Major scope items can be traced to an approved source.
- [ ] No material commercial scope change has been introduced without the required agreement.
- [ ] Confidential client material remains in the client project location.
- [ ] The scope is ready to support milestone planning.

## 12. Handoff to milestones

When scope is Approved:

1. Convert major deliverables into milestone candidates.
2. Sequence dependencies and client inputs.
3. Carry acceptance criteria into milestone and QA planning.
4. Carry open items forward with owners and status.
5. Identify milestone-level risks.
6. Establish the baseline against which future scope changes will be reviewed.

The approved scope is the delivery baseline. Milestones organize the work; they do not silently redefine the project scope.
